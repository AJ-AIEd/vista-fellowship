The method

Assessment as interpretable evidence of learning

VISTA is a methodology for examining and redesigning assessment where AI is available. It is being developed by Andrew Cajina, a high-school mathematics teacher and MEd researcher in AI and education.

Commitments

  • Assessment must provide interpretable evidence of what a student has learned.
  • Teachers keep professional and disciplinary judgment. The method structures that judgment; it does not replace it.
  • AI should extend meaningful human thinking, not substitute for it.
  • A polished product is not necessarily evidence of understanding.
  • Task design should preserve reasoning, interpretation, verification, revision, and intellectual agency.
  • A decision to exclude AI can be as defensible as a decision to include it.
  • Evidence requirements should be proportionate. More documentation is not better evidence.
  • AI literacy includes ethics, limitations, bias, verification, and questions of power.

Why prediction comes first

In the Initial Audit the teacher works without AI and commits to a prediction about what a tool could do with the task. In the routine that follows, they test it. The gap between the two is where most of the learning happens: a teacher who is surprised by the result has found out something specific about their task.

This order is a design choice informed by practice. It has not been tested against alternatives.

The AI–Human Thinking Routine (AI-HTR)

Each move starts with the teacher’s own judgment before any AI is consulted: what can AI do with this task, how much of its output can be trusted by the standards of the subject, and what would happen with these particular students. The routine’s final move, deciding who does what, is where the Redesign stage begins.

The wider journey and the assessment cycle

VISTA holds five arcs of professional learning: relationship with AI, responsibility to students, learning design, enactment and evidence, and professional and collective learning. Exploration and dialogue can precede an informed audit. The six-stage assessment cycle is one practical workflow within that journey; the arcs are not extra stages or competence scores.

Two instruments inside the audit

How much thinking could be handed over

Teachers estimate whether AI could take over the intended thinking fully, substantially, partially, or minimally. Each estimate leads to different fixed guidance, organised by five kinds of learning: procedural, conceptual, evaluative, creative, and embodied or tacit. “Minimally” is treated as a claim to examine, not a pass.

SAIL: five AI-use levels

SAIL is the local name used here for options adapted from the Artificial Intelligence Assessment Scale (AIAS), developed by Mike Perkins, Leon Furze, Jasper Roe and Jason MacVaugh (2024). These local labels are retained for the current tasks; they are not the current official AIAS wording. Read the original AIAS research and the current official framework. Attribution does not establish permission for commercial reuse; rights remain under review.

No AI, Ideate, Edit, Evaluate, Co-Create. The levels describe what AI may do in a task. They are not a ladder of quality, and different parts of one task can sit at different levels.

What VISTA does not claim

VISTA is not validated. It is a framework in development, in use with a small pilot group, and its effects on teacher judgment and student learning have not been measured.

The feedback in the tool is predetermined. It responds to the options a teacher selects, not to what they write.

A separate instrument, a baseline of teachers’ AI competencies based on UNESCO’s framework, is used in the author’s facilitated work. It is not part of the VISTA audit and is not included in this tool.
